## MMA vs. MIG vs. TIG: Technique Comparison

This is where the **Welding Simulator realistic game** earns its premise. The three processes are not cosmetic skins; in real shops they solve different problems, and the game lists all three as core techniques.

| Technique | How it works in the real world | Best suited to | Learning curve | In-game role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MMA (stick) | Flux-coated electrode melts into the joint; slag must be chipped off | Outdoor work, thicker steel, windy conditions | Moderate — forgiving setup, unforgiving technique | Practical starting point for structural jobs |
| MIG (wire feed) | Wire electrode fed through a gun with shielding gas | Sheet metal, fast production, thinner stock | Easiest to start, tricky to master on thin material | Speed option for frames and panels |
| TIG | Tungsten electrode with a separate filler rod and gas shield | Thin metals, precision joints, visible work | Steepest — two hands, foot control, patience | Precision test for advanced builds |

### MMA: the forgiving starting point
Stick welding is the least fussy process to set up, which makes it a sensible first discipline in a career mode. The trade-off is that you cannot hide a bad arc: travel speed and arc length show up immediately in the bead profile.

### MIG: speed with a cleanup bill
MIG lays down metal quickly, which is why it dominates light fabrication. In a game context, that speed is a trap for new players — moving too fast on thin sheet is how you blow through a panel. Treat MIG as your production process once your fit-up is consistent.

### TIG: the precision benchmark
TIG is the process that separates hobbyists from professionals. It is slower, demands coordination between both hands, and rewards clean metal. If the **Welding Simulator realistic game** models heat input even loosely, TIG will be where most players spend their practice hours.

A few practical tips for the progression loop:

- **Finish clean, not fast.** A rework costs more time than a slow pass.
