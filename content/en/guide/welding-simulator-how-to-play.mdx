## MMA, MIG, and TIG: What Each Method Actually Asks of You

Three processes are confirmed, and they are not interchangeable skins. In real welding — which this sim is explicitly modeling — each one has its own setup, its own rhythm, and its own failure modes. Learning all three is the core skill curve of the game.

| Method | How it works | Difficulty for newcomers | Typically used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MMA (stick) | A flux-coated electrode burns to form the weld; slag is chipped off afterward | Moderate | Thicker stock, rougher structural joints, outdoor-style work |
| MIG | Wire feeds continuously through a gun with shielding gas | Easiest to get a decent result | Sheet metal, long continuous runs, frames |
| TIG | A tungsten electrode creates the arc while filler is added by hand | Hardest to control | Thin metal, visible joints, precision work |

**MMA** rewards a steady hand and patience. Striking and maintaining the arc is the first hurdle, and restarting mid-bead is where most beginners struggle. It's forgiving on thicker, less-than-perfect metal, which makes it a reasonable confidence builder.

**MIG** is the friendliest entry point because the wire feeds itself — you focus on travel speed, angle, and gas coverage. The classic beginner error is running too cold and laying a bead that sits on top of the metal instead of fusing into it. Turn up the power until the bead flattens and ties into both edges.

**TIG** is two-handed work: one hand on the torch, one adding filler. It produces the cleanest-looking welds and punishes contamination harder than the other two. If the metal isn't clean, the arc wanders and the bead turns ugly.

**Practical tip:** before you commit to a paid project, burn a handful of practice beads on scrap. It's the cheapest calibration session you'll get, and it mirrors how real shops warm up.

## Step-by-Step Welding Simulator How to Play Walkthrough for Your First Session

Here's a sensible order of operations for session one. The exact interface isn't public yet, but the workflow follows the announced feature set and standard shop practice.

| Step | What to do | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Set up your station: clear bench, stock within reach, tools laid out | Fumbling mid-weld ruins a bead faster than bad technique |
| 2 | Pick one method and stay with it for the session | Switching processes constantly resets your muscle memory |
| 3 | Cut and fit your pieces before striking an arc | Gaps and misalignment can't be welded shut cleanly |
| 4 | Run practice beads on scrap plate | A cheap way to calibrate your hand before a paid job |
| 5 | Tack the assembly first, then weld in short runs | Tacking controls distortion and keeps parts aligned |
| 6 | Inspect the bead, then clean slag and spatter | Inspection is how quality gets judged, in-game and in real shops |
| 7 | Bank the payout and reinvest in one upgrade | Compounding gear beats scattered spending every time |

The most common mistake in simulation games of this type is skipping step three. Fit-up is unglamorous, but a two-millimeter gap will beat a perfect bead every time. Measure, mark, cut, and dry-fit before the torch comes out.

| Beginner mistake | What it costs you | The fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Welding dirty, painted, or rusty metal | Porous, weak welds | Grind or clean the surface first |
| Too much heat on thin stock | Burn-through and warping | Lower the power, move faster, switch to MIG or TIG |
| Rushing the fit-up | Gaps you can't close | Measure twice, tack before welding |
| Never practicing between jobs | Slow improvement, wasted contracts | Ten minutes of scrap beads before each session |
| Spending every payout on gear | No cash buffer for bigger jobs | Upgrade one thing at a time, keep a reserve |
